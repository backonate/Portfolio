BuE. coli

Sept 2025 - April 2026 | UBC iGEM, Best 3rd Year Project

BuE. coli was our UBC 3rd year IGEN capstone: an autonomous buoy that collects, incubates, and tests water samples for E. coli, then remotely transmits water-quality data to stakeholders such as Vancouver Coastal Health. The project won Best 3rd Year Project.

Metro Vancouver's standard beach monitoring relies on weekly manual sampling and the culture-based Colilert-18 method, which requires roughly 18 hours of incubation. That turnaround means results may not reflect rapidly changing water conditions driven by rainfall, runoff, sewage contamination, wildlife, and tides. Our goal was a system that could sample and test daily without manual intervention, closing that gap with more frequent data.

On the mechanical team, I designed and built the buoy's casing, frame, and incubation system. Fitting mechanical, electrical, and detection subsystems into a compact, stable, waterproof enclosure was the central constraint driving the design. My work included 3D modelling the frame, lid, internal mounting system, heat-insert placements, and component brackets, and designing for stability and flotation under the full system load. I also built a closed-loop heating system using a PTC heating element and thermistor to hold samples at approximately 37°C through the 24-hour incubation period, and designed and tested custom test-tube caps to prevent leakage from vibration and tilting.

Testing and iteration ran throughout: I validated the buoy under load and confirmed its stability against small waves, vibration, and pump forces, updating the mechanical design continuously as the electrical and detection subsystems evolved.

I was also responsible for researching rapid E. coli detection methods, comparing qPCR, bacterial culturing, microfluidics, and fluorescence-based detection before selecting a fluorescence method developed by the American Society for Microbiology. This involved incubating E. coli samples in EC MUG broth and using 365 nm UV light with a photodiode to measure fluorescence. In final lab testing, our sensor readings tracked the trend between higher and lower E. coli concentrations, verified against qPCR testing of the same samples.

The final prototype integrated water intake and filtration, sample dispensing, incubation, fluorescence-based detection, remote data transmission, solar-assisted power, and a floating mechanical platform into a single autonomous system.
